STYLE: THE PRINTED LEGGED ROBOT. The project chose this style: a small robot, printed around hobby or bus servos, boards, a battery and sensors, that stands and walks on legs. Its rules add to the design rules above, and where they say more, they win.

- THE KIT. Unless the brief says otherwise, start from the catalog's small-robot parts: `lib.board("esp32-devkitc-v4")` as the controller (`"pi-zero-2-w"` when the task needs Linux, `"pi-5"` for a camera or vision); `lib.board("pca9685-adafruit-rev-c")` when there are more PWM servos than the controller drives cleanly; `lib.board("bno085-adafruit-4754")` as the IMU, rigid on the body near its centre, its X axis forward; `lib.battery("gensace-gea2s100045d")` (2S LiPo) through `lib.board("pololu-d36v50f6")` (6 V) for the servos; a front sensor such as `lib.board("pololu-vl53l1x-3415")` or `"rpi-camera-module-3"`. Where a joint carries real load or the policy needs its angle, `lib.servo("sts3215")`, a bus servo that reports position and load on one daisy-chained cable, with `voltage=6.0` on its `.actuator` because a full 2S pack is over its rating. A stock PWM servo reports nothing back: its angle is a policy input only through a declared `joint_encoder`, which means tapping its potentiometer -- record that. The battery is often the heaviest part: carry it low and central.
- SERVOS SIT IN THE LINK. A link that carries a servo wraps its `.bay()` with a printable wall (about 1.6-2.4 mm) and is split where the servo drops in, its tabs screwed down at `servo.spec["mount_holes"]`; cut a tabbed servo with `servo.bay(ledge=4)` so the lead-side screw has material to bite. A foot pad such as `lib.foot_pad("essentra-462178")` screws onto a printed foot through its `.bay()`.
- ONE OF TWO FINISHES. In step 1, the concept fixes the palette -- one shell colour, graphite, at most one accent -- and one of two finishes, with the reason in DECISIONS.md:
  - EXPOSED MECHANISM: the actuators, boards, battery and cable run are visible, square to the frame, on a centreline or a symmetric grid, each held by its own screws or bay, so the layout itself is the design.
  - PANELLED HARD SURFACE: the parts are enclosed by flat panels, chamfers, a real seam between a tub and a lid, a bolted hatch and visible fasteners -- crisp rather than one soft skin. At its plainest it is a few well-proportioned volumes with nothing decorative.
- TWO MATERIALS AND ONE SMALL ACCENT. The accent marks what works -- horn caps, a cradle, the feet, a power cable, a status light, a sensor bezel -- and stays small enough to mark it; `{{look}}`'s measures count the materials.
- NOT THE MASCOT BOX. A large, soft, rounded box over short or thin limbs, with blocks hanging under it, is the one archetype to avoid above all others: it hides the structure and reads as a toy. If the design drifts towards it, go back to step 4.
- NO FACE. Eyes, a visor or a mouth are costume on a machine. The front may carry a real sensor instead -- a camera, a range sensor, or a slot cut for one -- held on the body's forward axis, or the controller can be the focal point. One sensor part reads better than a pair of round lenses, which reads as eyes.
- JOINTS ARE FEATURES. Each actuated axis reads as one round feature -- a cap, hub or drum concentric with the axis that covers its horn -- so a horn reads as a hub rather than a bare arm, and one joint design serves the whole robot. The cap goes where the horn is, between the servo's case top and the part it drives: make the driven part's hub the cap, of radius the horn's `.spec["arm_reach_mm"]` plus a printable wall, cut with `horn.body` so the horn and its screw sit inside, with a skirt reaching down near the case top. The shorter the horn that carries the link, the smaller the cap: on a micro servo, `servo.horn("cross")` makes a cap about two thirds the size a `single_arm` horn needs. Close the servo's other face with the limb that wraps the case rather than a second disc, so each axis shows one feature, not a stack.
- LIMBS CARRY LOAD, AND FEET ARE PARTS. A walking leg is a beam that follows its load path: the bending moment falls from the hip to the foot, so the section tapers in both directions, depth as well as width -- a plate of one thickness cut to a tapering outline is still a flat bar edge-on. Shelled or ribbed, never a thin stick, and ending in a designed foot, never the cut end of a bar. Check it: `{{look}}` `focus` on one leg, from the side and from the front.
- LEGS ARE LONG AGAINST THEIR JOINTS. A leg reads as a limb when its segments, not its joints, are most of what you see, and a longer shin gives a longer stride for the same servo. Size the leg from the step you want and the joint caps, not the joints from the leg; a thigh and a shin each a few cap diameters long between axes (about 2.5 is a good start), the shin the longest, and a hip link no longer than its two servos need. The standing pose depends on how the leg hangs. A SPRAWLED LEG -- one that reaches out sideways before it comes down, a spider's or a hexapod's -- stands with the thigh running out level or a little above and the shin coming down to the foot. An UPRIGHT LEG under the body, a biped's, stands with the thigh coming down to a slightly bent knee; the level-thigh rule is not for it. Either way the leg reads as one line from hip to foot, never a knee folded up high above the body or caps crowding each other. Check it with `{{look}}` `focus` on one leg.
- THE LOOK OF A LIMB. A thigh or shin is a plate that tapers along its length, widening towards the joint that loads it most, with a lightening window that follows the same taper, so the material sits where the bending is; every joint axis it carries ends in a round boss concentric with the axis.
- FEET ARE COMPACT HULLS WITH A FLAT STRIP. A foot is a compact hull, printed in the accent as its own part, because every gram at the end of the leg costs swing torque, a large sole catches the floor in the swing, and a wide one meets the other foot. Stability on one foot comes from the strip -- two contact lines and the distance between them -- and from where it sits under the centre of mass, never from the foot's area. A walking robot stands on one foot at a time, and an underside that is one round keel balances on a knife edge, so training finds a shuffle instead of a step: give it two keels with a flat strip between them, across the direction the foot must not tip, built from the same capsules and boxes as its collision shapes, and centre the strip under the measured centre of mass. COMPACT IS MEASURED: record the standing height (floor to the top of the body) and the sole's length and width against it in DECISIONS.md. A sole about a quarter of the height long and an eighth wide is a good start; past that, show the swing still clears the floor and the feet still pass, by the sweep, never only by eye.
- HIPS WIDE ENOUGH FOR THE FEET TO PASS. The feet meet when a leg rolls inward, so the inward roll limit comes from the angle where they meet, measured. MEASURE WHERE THE FEET MEET, and bracket it outward from the standing pose: set the inward limit one sweep step past the standing pose, sweep, and read the hip roll's row from `{{inspect}} scope=clearance path=/clearance_sweep/joints`; while it is clear, move the limit out one step and sweep again, until a foot or shin meets the other leg. That gives the last clear angle and the first contact angle, and the pair that met. Never open the range wide and read `first_contact` off one sweep: it is the first contacting sample counted from the range's lower limit, so on an inward side that runs negative it is the deepest contact, not where contact starts. Set the inward limit short of the first contact by at least two sweep steps, because contact can begin anywhere between two samples, and write both angles in DECISIONS.md. A limit picked first and found clear only proves it safe, not how much room is left. Set the hip spacing and the limit together, inward much tighter than outward, and measure again whenever a foot grows wider.
- THE STEP IS WHAT IS PAID. Defend the target speed from the stride and the servos' speed at the voltage they run at, and pick one at which full steps are the easy way, because a target a shuffle reaches trains a shuffle. Pay for the swing itself -- the lifted foot rising and advancing relative to the planted one -- and charge a planted foot for sliding, so a step earns more than any other way of moving the body. Charge the hips' common-mode pitch -- their mean angle away from the stance -- so the legs swing in antiphase and one leg does not take all the steps, which turns the robot.
- WHEN YOU LOOK, ALSO NAME a thin stick leg, a short leg under crowded joint caps, a bar end for a foot, a large, flat or single-keel foot, feet that meet when a hip rolls in, a face, a horn you can see and a soft box.
