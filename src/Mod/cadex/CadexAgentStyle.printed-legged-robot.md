STYLE: THE PRINTED LEGGED ROBOT. The project chose this style: a small robot, printed around hobby or bus servos, boards, a battery and sensors, that stands and walks on legs. Its rules add to the design rules above, and where they say more, they win.

- ONE OF TWO FINISHES. In step 1, the concept also fixes the palette -- one shell colour, graphite, at most one accent -- and the finish, which is one of two. Pick the one that suits this machine and record why in DECISIONS.md:
  - EXPOSED MECHANISM: the actuators, boards, battery and cable run are visible, square to the frame, on a centreline or a symmetric grid, each held by its own screws or bay, so the layout itself is the design.
  - PANELLED HARD SURFACE: the parts are enclosed by flat panels, chamfers, a real seam between a tub and a lid, a bolted hatch and visible fasteners -- crisp, never one soft skin. At its plainest it is a few well-proportioned volumes with nothing decorative.
- TWO MATERIALS AND ONE SMALL ACCENT. The accent covers well under a tenth of the surface: horn caps, a cradle, the feet, a power cable, a status light, a sensor bezel.
- NOT THE MASCOT BOX. The body is never a large, soft, rounded box over short or thin limbs with blocks hanging under it. That one archetype is the thing to avoid above all others; if your design is drifting towards it, go back to step 4.
- NO FACE. Do not give the machine eyes, a visor, a mouth or any face. The front may carry a real sensor instead -- a camera, a range sensor, or a slot cut for one -- as a held part on the body's forward axis, or the controller can be the focal point. Prefer one sensor part or one slot to a pair of round lenses, which reads as eyes.
- JOINTS ARE FEATURES. Every actuated axis reads as the same round feature -- a cap, hub or drum concentric with the axis and at least as wide as the horn it covers -- so a horn reads as a hub, never a bare arm. One joint design serves the whole robot. The cap goes where the horn is: the horn sits between the servo's case top and the part it drives, so a disc on the servo's far face or beside the hub leaves the horn in view. Make the driven part's hub the cap: a disc of radius at least the horn's `.spec["arm_reach_mm"]` plus a 1.6 mm wall, cut with `horn.body` so the horn and its screw sit inside it, with a skirt reaching down past the horn to within 1 mm of the case top. A joint is one cap, as small as covers its horn: drive the link with the shortest horn that carries it -- on a micro servo `servo.horn("cross")`, whose 10.2 mm reach makes a cap about two thirds the diameter a `single_arm` horn needs -- and size the cap to that reach plus the wall, no larger. Do not add a second disc on the servo's other face: close that side with the limb that wraps the case, so each axis shows one round feature, never a stack of discs.
- LIMBS CARRY LOAD, AND FEET ARE PARTS. A walking leg is a tapered, shelled or ribbed beam that follows the load path, about 60% of its hip section or less near the foot, never a thin stick. Taper the section in both directions, its depth as well as its width, so the limb narrows seen from the side as well as from above: a plate of one thickness cut to a tapering outline is still a flat bar edge-on. It ends in a designed foot -- a pad, a cap or a plate, or a wheel with a tyre -- never the cut end of a bar.
- LEGS ARE LONG AGAINST THEIR JOINTS. Size a walking leg from its joint caps, not the joints from the leg: between axes, the thigh (femur) and the shin (tibia) are each at least 2.5 times the joint cap's diameter, the shin is the longest segment, and a hip (coxa) link is no longer than its two servos need. The standing pose depends on how the leg hangs. A SPRAWLED LEG -- one that reaches out sideways from the body before it comes down, a spider's or a hexapod's -- stands with the thigh running out level or a little above level and the shin coming down to the foot. An UPRIGHT LEG under the body, a biped's, stands with the thigh coming down from the hip to a slightly bent knee; the level-thigh rule is not for it. Either way the leg reads as one line from hip to foot: never a knee folded up high above the body, and never joints so close together that their caps crowd each other. Check it with `{{look}}` `focus` on one leg: the limbs, not the joints, are most of what you see.
- THE LOOK OF A LIMB. A thigh or shin is a plate that tapers along its length, widening towards the joint that loads it most, with a lightening window cut through it that follows the same taper, so the material sits where the bending is. Every joint axis it carries ends in a round boss concentric with the axis. A shin also tapers in depth, so it narrows seen from the side as well as from the front.
- FEET ARE COMPACT HULLS WITH A FLAT STRIP. A foot is a compact hull, no bigger than the robot needs to stand on, never a large or flat plate, printed in the accent colour as its own part. COMPACT HAS A NUMBER: measure the robot's standing height, floor to the top of the body, and keep each sole no longer than a quarter of it and no wider than an eighth of it; record the height and both ratios in DECISIONS.md. A sole past either bound is a large flat plate, whatever it is built from. Stability on one foot comes from the strip -- the two contact lines and the distance between them -- and from where the strip sits under the centre of mass, never from the foot's area: a bigger sole adds mass at the end of the leg, catches the floor in the swing and meets the other foot. A walking robot stands on one foot at a time, and a foot whose underside is one round keel balances on a knife edge: it gives no base across itself, and training finds a shuffle instead of a step. Give the underside two keels with a flat strip between them -- two contact lines across the direction the foot must not tip -- built from the same capsules and boxes its collision shapes are. Widen the base with that strip, never by growing the foot. Centre the strip under the centre of mass, front to back, as measured.
- HIPS WIDE ENOUGH FOR THE FEET TO PASS. The feet meet when a leg rolls inward, and the inward roll limit comes from the angle where they meet, measured, never chosen first. MEASURE WHERE THE FEET MEET, and bracket it outward from the standing pose: set the inward roll limit one sweep step past the standing pose, sweep, and read the hip roll's joint row from `{{inspect}} scope=clearance path=/clearance_sweep/joints`; while the row is clear, move the inward limit out one sweep step and sweep again, until a foot or shin meets the other leg. That gives the last clear angle and the first contact angle, and the pair that met. Never open the range wide and read `first_contact` off one sweep: it is the first contacting sample counted from the range's lower limit, so on an inward side that runs negative it is the deepest contact, not where contact starts, and a limit set from it lies inside the collision. Set the inward limit short of the first contact by at least two sweep steps, and write both angles in DECISIONS.md: the last clear angle and the first contact. A limit picked first and then found clear by the sweep is not derived from it: the sweep only proves that the limit is safe, not how much room is left. Set the hip spacing and the limit together from that angle, inward much tighter than outward, and measure again and widen the hips whenever a foot grows wider.
- THE STEP IS WHAT IS PAID. A target speed so low that a shuffle reaches it trains a shuffle: pick one at which full steps are the easy way, within the servos' speed at the voltage they run at. Pay for the swing itself -- the lifted foot rising and advancing relative to the planted one -- and charge a planted foot for sliding, so a step earns more than any other way of moving the body. Charge the hips' common-mode pitch -- the two hips' mean angle away from the stance -- so the legs swing in antiphase about the stance and one leg does not take all the steps, which turns the robot.
- WHEN YOU LOOK, ALSO NAME a thin stick leg, a short leg under crowded joint caps, a bar end for a foot, a foot that is large, flat, past a quarter of the standing height long or an eighth wide, or a single round keel, feet that meet when a hip rolls in, a face, a horn you can see and a soft box.
