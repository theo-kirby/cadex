A named style for the agent guidance (ADR-560, ADR-626): the robot creature,
a machine that takes an animal's or a character's form. A project chooses it
in its agent.json (`cadex style --project DIR creature`); the guidance overlay
tells an agent to choose it when the brief names an animal. It adds to the
base and never restates it. Since ADR-652 it also carries what the base said
only of animals (the moving anatomy, the QDD tiers), and states its rules as
principles with reasons and checks rather than fixed counts. Its evidence is
the owner's rated reference set and the blind-rated baseline sweep of
2026-10-09 (docs/DESIGN-LANGUAGE.md, the style's section). Since ADR-679 the
form comes first and is the animal's, made by panels over machinery and
formers (the panel-cat run of 2026-10-13: a cat whose form fell out of its
mechanism read as a generic quadruped). Same format as
the base: everything below the marker line is the guidance, verbatim, with

STYLE: THE ROBOT CREATURE. The project chose this style: a machine that takes the form of an animal or a character -- a raptor, a bird, a cat, a dog, a lizard -- built as a real robot of actuators, frame, panels and sensors, whose likeness comes from its form, its proportions and the way it moves, never from a costume over a machine. Its rules add to the design rules above, and where they say more, they win.

- THE ANATOMY IS THE JOINT LIST. A machine in an animal's form moves like one, because its likeness lives in how it moves as much as in its outline. In step 1, write the animal's moving anatomy as a table in DECISIONS.md -- region, joints and their axes, the actuator for each, the range -- before you choose a part, starting from the animal rather than from the machine you would build anyway: what does its neck, head, jaw, tail, spine, arms, toes or wings do, and how many joints does that take? A long neck or tail usually bends in more than one place, and a jaw that is part of the character opens. A region you leave rigid gets a measured reason -- a mass, a torque or a collision you computed, never "decoration" or "simpler". Check it: declare every region with `assembly.anatomy(region, components, reason=...)` (the `reason=` only on a rigid one) and read the build's anatomy block, whose verdict is `complete` only when every region moves or carries its reason; acknowledge every `rigid_appendages` entry it names. Standing up does not settle the joint count.
- SIZE THE ACTUATOR TO THE JOINT. The joints that carry the body take the large actuators; a neck, head, jaw, tail, arm or toe carries far less and takes the smallest actuator that holds its load, computed from the segment's mass and lever and written in docs/actuators.md. An extra joint then costs the light actuator's mass, not the heavy one's. For a dynamic machine of several kilograms, `lib.qdd(sku)` carries these drives: `"cubemars-ak70-10"` or `"cubemars-ak80-9-v3"`, about half a kilogram each, for the joints that carry the machine; `"cubemars-ak10-9-v3"` (940 g, 18 N·m continuous) where those run past their continuous rating at a girdle joint of a running machine; `"robstride-rs04"` (1420 g, 120 N·m peak, 40 continuous; front-face mounting only) for the hips, knees, elbows and spine of a machine of about 40 kg; `"robstride-rs00"` (310 g, 14 N·m peak), `"cubemars-ak60-6-v3"` (380 g) or `"cubemars-ak45-10-v3"` (262 g) for the lighter ones; `"robstride-rs05"` (191 g, 5.5 N·m peak), the smallest, for a neck, jaw, tail or toe; smaller joints may take a bus servo. A QDD is placed by its output face with the joint axis as `direction`; `.actuator(joint)` and `.joint_dynamics(joint)` model it, and `hold = qdd.mounting()` gives `hold.stator` and `hold.output`, each with `.screws` to place and `.holes` to cut. QDDs run from 24-48 V over CAN, and the catalog carries neither a pack at those voltages nor a transceiver: record the power and bus you assume. Mount heavy drives near the body and drive a distal joint through a pushrod or a cable where the mass at the tip would cost more than the linkage.
- THE ACTUATOR IS THE JOINT, AND IT SHOWS. In the rated references the drive reads as the joint: a round housing grown around it (`lib.housing`), concentric with the axis, with its face or a ring in the accent. A drive buried in a panel shaped like a muscle, or a joint hidden under a bellows or a skin, reads as a costume. Repeating one housing design across the machine, scaled to each actuator, is what makes it read as one product.
- THE FORM IS THE ANIMAL'S, AND IT COMES FIRST. A robot creature reads as its animal because its outer form is that animal's, and a form left to fall out of the mechanism reads as a generic machine, however good the mechanism. So after the parts are chosen and before you build structure, sculpt the form as the animal's masses, one `lib.form(stations)` each with stations every few centimetres, never from the box the parts happen to fill: for a big cat, a short round skull with its brow, cheekbones and short muzzle, ears, a thick neck rising from the chest, a shoulder blade standing above the back line at the withers, a deep ribcage with its keel, a tucked waist, the haunch, and each limb segment -- the forearm thick at the elbow and slim at the wrist, the hind leg's long hock. Make the trunk one form -- one `lib.form` whose stations run from the base of the neck through the chest, the waist and the pelvis to the root of the tail -- so the back line and the belly line are each one curve; the head, the neck and each limb segment are forms of their own. Where masses meet, sink one into the next so they fuse into one surface. Build the forms alone first and `{{look}}` at `right`, `front` and `hero`, then put each beside the reference: they must read as the animal before a drive goes inside. Then place the mechanism inside the form, and where a drive does not fit, move the joint, choose a smaller part or change the form a little -- recorded in DECISIONS.md -- rather than letting the form swell round it. A drive standing out of the shoulder or the haunch reads as a machine wearing plates: the drives turning a shoulder blade, a hip or an abduction sit inside the girdle's mass, and only a housing's face may show at a joint.
- PANELS MAKE THE FORM; MACHINERY AND STRUCTURE FILL IT. The look this style aims at is a dense machine -- drives, linkages, cable runs, struts -- dressed in many smooth panels that together make the animal's shape. Cut those panels from the form itself: `part.skin([forms...], regions=[...], openings=[...], frame=..., screw=lib.bolt(size, length))` makes one piece per region, the form's own surface all the way round, so a shoulder plate wraps the shoulder and a shin plate the shin. A region is a solid whose crossing with the form is the panel's outline: draw a plate's outline in the side view as a closed `part.bspline` and extrude it across the body, or use a small `lib.form` or sphere for an oval plate; regions earlier in the list win where they overlap, `gap=` apart. Split along the anatomy, not along a grid: a skull of brow, cheek, muzzle and jaw plates; plates down the neck; a shoulder blade over the shoulder; the ribcage in two or three bands; the haunch; plates on each limb segment. Open the skin where the animal bends -- at the joints, the shoulder, the belly, the throat -- so the dark machinery shows there. Where a leg, a drum or a link swings through a plate, open the plate round its path, `openings=[{"around": part, "motion": {...}}]` and the same in `keep_clear=`, rather than dropping the plate. The form stands off its machinery where the animal is fuller than the machine, so it needs support under it: `part.former(form, origin, normal, web=..., inset=thickness + clearance)` hoops where the pieces need carrying, welded to the frame or link that carries them and fused into the skin's `frame=`. Read `fit.panels`: its `forms` block gives each form's `covered_fraction` -- the trunk, the girdles and the head mostly covered, with the openings chosen; a form in `under_covered` reads as bands, not armour -- and its `outside` block names each part standing out of the forms, which the previous rule moves inside; each piece's span to the support says whether it is carried; a form over air with nothing under it is the costume this style forbids. Where a cover wraps machinery rather than a designed form, `part.envelope(over=[...], form=[...])` and `part.panel` remain; name what each covers in `covers=`.
- EVERY REGION CARRIES ITS PANELS, LIMBS INCLUDED. Panels belong to the link they move with: an upper-arm plate is welded to the upper arm, a shin plate to the shin, a skull plate to the head, so a joint opens a gap between them rather than tearing a skin. Each limb segment is a structural link under its plate, tapered to its load, with the plate shaped to the animal's limb over it. A region left bare is a test rig; a region that is all shell, with no machinery showing anywhere, is a costume.
- SEGMENTS, NOT A SKIN, WHERE IT BENDS. A neck or a tail that bends is a chain of discrete segments, each its own part on its own joint, overlapping like plates or parted by a visible joint ring, tapering along the chain, rather than one loft over its whole length, which cannot bend where the joints are.
- THE CHARACTER IS A REAL SENSOR. The head carries the machine's real forward sensor -- a camera or a range sensor -- held by its own screws, and its bezel, a ring in the accent, is what reads as the eye: one sensor, or a matched pair where the part is a stereo camera. Set it where the animal's eye is, recessed under the brow and no larger against the skull than the animal's eye: a ring that fills the face reads as a cartoon. Painted eyes, a visor with dots or a mouth drawn on a panel are decoration, and read as a toy rather than a machine.
- TWO TONES AND ONE FUNCTIONAL ACCENT. A light shell and a dark mechanism, or the reverse, and an accent on what works -- joint rings, the sensor bezel, a power cable, foot pads -- kept small enough that it marks those features rather than decorating the body; a stripe marks nothing.
- LIKENESS BY SILHOUETTE AND PROPORTION, CHECKED AGAINST THE ANIMAL. Take the proportions from the animal -- its limb segment ratios and how many segments each leg has (a cat's forelimb of shoulder blade, upper arm, forearm and short pastern, its hind leg of thigh, shin and a long hock standing behind; a bird's or a raptor's digitigrade leg with a long tarsus), its neck and tail against its body, its head size, its posture -- and record them and where you took them in DECISIONS.md. A leg with fewer segments than the animal's, or one bent the other way, reads as another animal. Give each joint the range the animal's gait uses and read the build's sweep: a panel that cannot clear its joint through that range is cut back at the joint, never the range shrunk to fit the panel. Check it: `{{look}}` at `right`, `front` and `hero`; the side silhouette alone should name the animal, and the head alone should name it from the front. When the brief gives a reference image, read it again after every look, put your `hero` beside it, name region by region where your form departs from it -- head shape, neck set, shoulder mass, back line, haunch, leg thickness, stance, how much of each region is panelled -- and fix the worst first.
- THE BAR IS A REAL PRODUCT. Finish it as a real machine is finished -- consistent radii, housings that read as cast or machined, ordered fasteners, a cable on a deliberate path -- the robot a company would ship, not a model of one.
- WHEN YOU LOOK, ALSO NAME a region of the anatomy table that does not move, a neck or tail that is one loft, a shell that floats over what it covers, a drive buried in a panel, a part that is costume rather than structure, a face, a region with no panels, a form that follows the machine rather than the animal, and a side silhouette that does not read as the animal.
- BEFORE YOU HAND OFF, `{{look}}` once more and put the `hero` and `right` views beside the reference; write in PROGRESS.md, region by region, where it still departs, with the `forms` and `outside` numbers. A departure you can still fix within the brief is the next revision, not a note in the hand-off.
- THE COVERS ARE NOT THE LAST STEP. A creature reads by its form, so land a first full set of form panels as soon as the parts are placed and the main structure stands, and refine mechanism and panels together after that; a design whose covers are still to come at the end is unfinished in the one way the owner sees first.
