STYLE: THE ROBOT CREATURE. The project chose this style: a machine that takes the form of an animal or a character -- a raptor, a bird, a cat, a dog, a lizard -- built as a real robot of actuators, frame, panels and sensors, whose likeness comes from its silhouette, its proportions and the way it moves, never from a costume over a machine. Its rules add to the design rules above, and where they say more, they win.

- THE ANATOMY IS THE JOINT LIST. A machine in an animal's form moves like one, because its likeness lives in how it moves as much as in its outline. In step 1, write the animal's moving anatomy as a table in DECISIONS.md -- region, joints and their axes, the actuator for each, the range -- before you choose a part, starting from the animal rather than from the machine you would build anyway: what does its neck, head, jaw, tail, spine, arms, toes or wings do, and how many joints does that take? A long neck or tail usually bends in more than one place, and a jaw that is part of the character opens. A region you leave rigid gets a measured reason -- a mass, a torque or a collision you computed, never "decoration" or "simpler". Check it: declare every region with `assembly.anatomy(region, components, reason=...)` (the `reason=` only on a rigid one) and read the build's anatomy block, whose verdict is `complete` only when every region moves or carries its reason; acknowledge every `rigid_appendages` entry it names. Standing up does not settle the joint count.
- SIZE THE ACTUATOR TO THE JOINT. The joints that carry the body take the large actuators; a neck, head, jaw, tail, arm or toe carries far less and takes the smallest actuator that holds its load, computed from the segment's mass and lever and written in docs/actuators.md. An extra joint then costs the light actuator's mass, not the heavy one's. For a dynamic machine of several kilograms, `lib.qdd(sku)` carries two tiers: `"cubemars-ak70-10"` or `"cubemars-ak80-9-v3"`, about half a kilogram each, for the joints that carry the machine, and `"cubemars-ak60-6-v3"` (380 g) or `"cubemars-ak45-10-v3"` (262 g) for the lighter ones; smaller joints may take a bus servo. A QDD is placed by its output face with the joint axis as `direction`; `.actuator(joint)` and `.joint_dynamics(joint)` model it, and `hold = qdd.mounting()` gives `hold.stator` and `hold.output`, each with `.screws` to place and `.holes` to cut. QDDs run from 24-48 V over CAN, and the catalog carries neither a pack at those voltages nor a transceiver: record the power and bus you assume. Mount heavy drives near the body and drive a distal joint through a pushrod or a cable where the mass at the tip would cost more than the linkage.
- THE ACTUATOR IS THE JOINT, AND IT SHOWS. In the rated references the drive reads as the joint: a round housing grown around it (`lib.housing`), concentric with the axis, with its face or a ring in the accent. A drive buried in a panel shaped like a muscle, or a joint hidden under a bellows or a skin, reads as a costume. Repeating one housing design across the machine, scaled to each actuator, is what makes it read as one product.
- SHELLS SIT ON THE MASSES; LINKS DO THE WORK. The torso, the head and the clusters of drives carry panels; the long members -- shins, neck and tail segments, arms -- are slender structural links, tapered to their load, that show their structure. A creature that is all shell is a costume; one with no panels is a test rig.
- PANELS WRAP THE MECHANISM. A panel may curve -- gently bent, form-fitting -- but each one is grown from what it covers (`lib.panel(over=[...])`), at a small clearance, thin, split at seams that follow the form and the access you need, and screwed to the frame that carries it. A panel sized by eye around nothing is the defect: an egg, a dome, a sausage or a cone over the electronics. Check it: the build reply's shell check names every shell that floats, is solid or is unmounted.
- SEGMENTS, NOT A SKIN, WHERE IT BENDS. A neck or a tail that bends is a chain of discrete segments, each its own part on its own joint, overlapping like plates or parted by a visible joint ring, tapering along the chain, rather than one loft over its whole length, which cannot bend where the joints are.
- THE CHARACTER IS A REAL SENSOR. The head carries the machine's real forward sensor -- a camera or a range sensor -- held by its own screws, and its bezel, a ring in the accent, is what reads as the eye: one sensor, or a matched pair where the part is a stereo camera. Painted eyes, a visor with dots or a mouth drawn on a panel are decoration, and read as a toy rather than a machine.
- TWO TONES AND ONE FUNCTIONAL ACCENT. A light shell and a dark mechanism, or the reverse, and an accent on what works -- joint rings, the sensor bezel, a power cable, foot pads -- kept small enough that it marks those features rather than decorating the body; a stripe marks nothing.
- LIKENESS BY SILHOUETTE AND PROPORTION. Take the proportions from the animal -- its limb segment ratios (a bird's or a raptor's digitigrade leg with a long tarsus, a cat's long back), its neck and tail length against its body, its head size, its posture -- and record them and where you took them in DECISIONS.md. Check it: `{{look}}` at `right`; the side silhouette alone should name the animal.
- THE BAR IS A REAL PRODUCT. Finish it as a real machine is finished -- consistent radii, housings that read as cast or machined, ordered fasteners, a cable on a deliberate path -- the robot a company would ship, not a model of one.
- WHEN YOU LOOK, ALSO NAME a region of the anatomy table that does not move, a neck or tail that is one loft, a shell that floats over what it covers, a drive buried in a panel, a part that is costume rather than structure, a face, and a side silhouette that does not read as the animal.
